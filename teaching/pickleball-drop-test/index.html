Pickleball Drop Test

Fairfax Pickleball Works produces tournament pickleballs. According to the USA Pickleball official equipment standard, a ball dropped from 78 inches onto a granite plate must bounce between 30 and 34 inches. No two balls bounce exactly the same. Every hour, quality control pulls 10 balls off the line and drop-tests them. At the end of each 10-hour day, the 100 tested balls tell us whether the line is capable; every hour, the average of the 10 goes on the control chart.

Sally's line. Over 100 days about 2.6% of her balls fall outside the limits, against about 0.25% of John's. Sally's wide spread sends balls past both limits. John's. The aim is one machine setting. At 32 inches his line is both accurate and consistent, and practically no ball falls outside the limits (Cpk = 2 / (3 × 0.35) = 1.90). Sally's line needs less variation, which usually means changing the process itself: materials, equipment, training. No single setting does that.

On the slides: capability index Cpk (slides 11–18)

Is the line capable?

A ball is a defect if it bounces outside 30–34 inches. Whether the line is capable comes down to two distances:

  • Safety distance: the room between the average bounce X̄ and the nearer limit.
  • The mistake: the distance from the average, in either direction, that balls stay within with a given probability (slide 13: k×σ, the actual deviation). 99.7% of balls stray less than 3σ, so at the 3σ level the mistake is 3σ.

The line is capable at the 3σ level if the 3σ mistake fits inside the safety distance. Run the day, then click Next under the histogram to build the picture step by step.

Moving a slider reruns Day 1 with the new setting, so the histogram and the picture update at once.

Predict, then run. Let the day run. Will the 3σ mistake fit inside the safety distance?

Show what happens

No. Today X̄ = 32.40 and σ = 0.80 inches. The nearer limit is UTL = 34, so the safety distance is 1.60 inches; the 3σ mistake is 3 × 0.80 = 2.40 inches. The mistake runs 0.80 inches past the upper limit: not capable at the 3σ level (Cpk = 1.60 / 2.40 = 0.67). On the LTL side it just fits (2.40 = 2.40). In the histogram, 2 of the 100 balls bounce above 34; in the long run about 2.4% of balls fall outside the limits*.

Then try. Move the aim to the target, 32 inches. Is the line capable now?

Show what happens

Not yet. Centering makes the safety distance 2.00 inches on both sides, but the 3σ mistake is still 2.40 inches (Cpk = 2.00 / 2.40 = 0.83). Fewer balls fall outside (about 1.2% instead of 2.4%*), but the mistake still does not fit.

Then try. Keep the aim at 32 and lower σ. How small must σ be for the 3σ mistake to fit?

Show what happens

The 3σ mistake must fit in the 2-inch safety distance: 3σ ≤ 2, so σ ≤ 2/3 = 0.666… inches (at σ = 0.67 the mistake is 2.01, just too long). At σ = 0.65 the mistake is 1.95 inches and fits (Cpk = 2.00 / 1.95 = 1.03); only about 0.2% of balls fall outside*. A smaller σ means a smaller mistake: reducing variation (better materials, equipment, training) is what makes a line capable.

The formula (slides 11–12)

A line is capable at the k×σ level if the k×σ mistake fits inside the limits on both sides:

X̄ + k × σ ≤ UTL and X̄ − k × σ ≥ LTL

The capability index puts this into one number:

Cpk = min{(X̄ − LTL) / (k × σ), (UTL − X̄) / (k × σ)}Cpk = min{X̄ − LTLk × σ,UTL − X̄k × σ}

The tops are the room to each limit (the smaller one is the safety distance); the bottom is the k×σ mistake. Cpk ≥ 1: capable at the k×σ level. Cpk < 1: not capable. On this tab, k = 3.

* The probability of a defect uses the normal curve, as in the starred question of the example (slide 16, solved on slide 18).

Keep the line in control

The plant has re-aimed the machine and improved the mold: average 32 inches, σ = 0.5 inches, so the line is capable (Cpk = 2 / (3 × 0.5) = 1.33). Now the job is to keep it that way. Every hour, the average of the 10 tested balls goes on the control chart. The control limits come from how this line normally behaves.

The patterns on slides 21–22. Use the buttons above the control chart: each one shows a day with that pattern and what caused it. The questions below let you make things go wrong yourself.

Predict, then run. Let the line run with nothing going wrong. Do the hourly averages stay on the central line?

Show what happens

No. They move up and down around it, but stay inside the control limits with no pattern: normal behavior (slide 21). That movement is common-cause variation. Nothing needs fixing.

Predict. Click Bad plastic batch under the factory. What will the chart show?

Show what happens

The next hour's average jumps above the upper control limit: one point out above (slide 21). Quality control investigates and finds the batch; once it is used up, the points return to normal.

Predict. Click Plant gets cold and let it run a few hours.

Show what happens

The averages drop below the central line and stay there. After about five hours that is five in a row below the central line, a signal (slide 22); a point may also fall below the lower control limit. Look at the histogram: almost all balls are still within 30–34. The chart warns before the problem makes bad balls, so the plant can act in time. Click Find the cause and fix it.

Then try. Oven runs hot, then (after fixing it) New operator. Give the new operator a day or two.

Show what happens

Oven runs hot: the averages climb hour after hour. Usually 2 to 4 hours after you click, a point goes above the upper control limit (slide 21); sometimes the last five points also form a rising trend (slide 22). New operator: the averages jump up and down. The first signal is usually one point outside a control limit, often within a couple of hours. Keep it running: usually within a day or two, points fall outside both control limits: erratic behavior (slide 22; the Erratic button above the chart shows such a day). The histogram widens and many balls fall outside 30–34.

Then try. Click Reset, speed up, and let the line run for a week with nothing going wrong. Does the chart ever signal?

Show what happens

Yes, now and then (on this line, first on Day 5). Each hourly average lands above or below the central line by chance, like a coin toss, so five in a row on one side, or five steadily rising, sometimes happens in a perfectly normal line: in this factory, on about 4 days in 10. Quality control investigates and finds nothing: a false alarm. A longer rule, such as seven in a row, gives fewer false alarms but warns later; the slides use five.

Then try. In control but not capable? (aim 32.4, σ = 0.8) and run a day with nothing going wrong.

Show what happens

The chart shows normal behavior all day: the line is in control. Yet 2 of the 100 tested balls bounce above 34 inches (about 2.4% in the long run*), so it is not capable. Control limits describe what the line normally does; the tolerance limits, 30 and 34, are what the customer requires. In control means only common causes are at work; capable means that common-cause variation fits the tolerance limits. This line needs a process change (aim and spread), not a hunt for special causes.

Challenges

  • With σ = 0.8, is there any aim that makes the line capable at 3σ?
    Show the answer

    No. The best you can do is aim at the middle, 32 inches, which gives Cpk = 2 / (3 × 0.8) = 0.83. With this much variation, aiming cannot fix the line; only a smaller σ can.

  • Aim at 32.4 with σ = 0.8. Which bad ball is more common: too dead (below 30) or too bouncy (above 34)?
    Show the answer

    Too bouncy, by far*: about 2.3% of balls bounce above 34 inches and about 0.1% below 30. The upper limit is only 2σ above the average (z = 2.0); the lower one is 3σ below it (z = −3.0).

  • Aim at 32 with σ = 0.5 and click Run 100 days. How many of the 10,000 tested balls fall outside 30–34?
    Show the answer

    Hardly any. Cpk = 1.33, so each limit is 4σ from the average; the normal curve* gives about 0.006% of balls, fewer than one ball in 10,000.

  • Can a line be capable but out of control?
    Show the answer

    Yes. Aim at 32 with σ = 0.5 (capable, Cpk = 1.33) and click Oven runs hot. Within a few hours the chart signals a problem, while almost every ball is still within 30–34. Capability is about how the process is designed; control is about running it day to day (slide 5). The chart lets the plant step in before the drift makes bad balls.

  • If a line is capable at the 3σ level, is it also capable at the 2σ level? And the other way round?
    Show the answer

    Yes: the 2σ mistake is shorter than the 3σ mistake, so if 3σ fits inside the safety distance, 2σ fits too (Cpk at 2σ is 1.5 times Cpk at 3σ). The other way round, not necessarily. Aim at 32 with σ = 0.8: at the 2σ level Cpk = 2 / (2 × 0.8) = 1.25, capable; at the 3σ level 2 / (3 × 0.8) = 0.83, not capable. Try both with the level k choice above.

How this simulation works
  • Each ball's bounce height is random: a normal distribution with the machine's aim as its average and the ball-to-ball variation as its standard deviation. The molding machine makes 600 balls an hour (the moving balls are only a picture of that).
  • Quality control tests one ball every 6 minutes: 10 per hour, 100 per 10-hour day (7 am to 5 pm). The test follows the USA Pickleball rule: drop the ball from 78 inches onto a granite plate; it must bounce 30 to 34 inches.
  • X̄ and σ use the formulas on slide 10 (σ divides by N). Cpk uses slides 11–13 with the k you choose. *The probability of a defect uses the normal curve, as in the starred question of the example (slide 16, solved on slide 18).
  • Control chart: one point per hour, the average of that hour's 10 tested balls. The central line is the machine's aim, and the control limits come from the line's normal variation, so they move only when you change the aim or σ, not when something goes wrong.
  • Signals (slides 21–22): a point above or below a control limit; five points in a row on one side of the central line; five points in a row each higher (or each lower) than the one before; erratic: among the last five points, at least one above the upper control limit and one below the lower control limit. Now and then a run or a trend of five happens by chance in a normal line; an investigation then finds nothing.
  • Special causes: bad plastic batch = the next hour's balls bounce 1.2 inches higher; plant gets cold = 0.3 inches lower; oven runs hot = 0.25 inches higher every hour, up to 3 inches; new operator = ball-to-ball variation 3 times larger. All but the batch last until you click Find the cause and fix it.
  • Pattern buttons (Control chart tab): each shows Day 1 of the line with aim 32 and σ = 0.5, with one cause (none for Normal) timed so the pattern ends at 5:00 pm. One point above: bad plastic batch from 4:00 pm. One point below: soft plastic batch from 4:00 pm (1.2 inches lower for one hour). Run: plant gets cold at noon. Trend: the air conditioning fails at noon (0.1 inches higher every hour, up to 1 inch). Erratic: new operator from 10:00 am. Their random numbers were picked so that each day shows its pattern and no unrelated signal; with other random numbers the same cause can look different.
  • Cpk practice: each new problem runs one full day on a line with random settings; X̄ and σ come from that day's 100 tested balls.
  • Run 100 days tests 10,000 balls with the current settings and no special causes, with the same random numbers every time. "Start over" goes back to Day 1 with the same balls; "New random balls" with new ones; "Reset" goes back to the tab's starting settings.
