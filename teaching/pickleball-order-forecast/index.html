Pickleball Order Forecast

Fairfax Pickleball Works, the plant from the Drop Test, sells tournament balls by the case (100 balls a case) to clubs and sporting-goods stores. Molding takes time, so at the end of every month the plant must decide how many cases to make for next month. Make too few and orders go unfilled; make too many and cases pile up in the warehouse. So each month the planner forecasts next month's orders from past orders. The chart shows the last three years of orders and the one month nobody knows yet: next month.

On the slides: time series and its components (slides 9–13)

What drives the orders?

The orders are a time series: one number per month, evenly spaced (slide 9). Three things move them:

  • Trend: a persistent upward or downward pattern over several years (slide 11). Here: the sport keeps growing.
  • Season: a regular up-and-down pattern within each year (slide 12). Here: clubs play outdoors in spring and summer.
  • Random: erratic, short, non-repeating changes (slide 13). Here: a rainy month, a tournament, a big club order.

Predict. Orders are high every summer and low every winter. Which component is that? How many seasons does the pattern have in one year?

Show the answer

The seasonal component: clubs play outdoors in spring and summer, every year. With monthly data and a pattern that repeats every year, each month is one "season", so the pattern has 12 of them (the Year–Month row of the table on slide 12). Turn on Show trend + season: the curve peaks in June–July and bottoms out in December–January every year. The bars bounce around it because of the random part; in Year 3, August had the most orders.

Predict. Each summer is higher than the one before. By how much do orders grow, roughly?

Show the answer

That is the trend: +10 cases a month, so each month is about 120 cases above the same month last year (12 × 10). Yearly totals grow by about 12 × 120 = 1,440 cases, give or take the random part: 7,895 cases in Year 1, 9,363 in Year 2, and 10,846 in Year 3. Turn on Show trend line: the straight line climbs steadily under the seasonal ups and downs.

Then try. Turn on Show the random part, then switch Random off. What is left?

Show the answer

With Random off, every bar sits exactly on the trend + season curve: what is left can be predicted perfectly. The random part is what no forecasting method can predict (slide 13), which is why forecasts are seldom perfect (slide 5). Click any month to see its order split into level + trend + season + random.

On the slides: simple and weighted moving averages (slides 14–30)

Average the past

A moving average forecasts next month as the average of the last n months (slide 16). Each month the window moves one month forward: the oldest month drops out and the newest comes in. The shaded months on the chart are the window for the selected month. The weighted moving average gives each month in the window its own weight (slide 25).

The answers below are for the starting settings: 3 and 6 months, weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2. brings them back.

Predict, then step. Go to June, Year 3 (1,009 cases ordered). Which forecast is closer: the 3-month or the 6-month moving average?

Show the answer

The 3-month: (1,000 + 883 + 819) / 3 = 900.67 cases, off by 108.33. The 6-month average still includes the low months of last winter: 809.00, off by 200.00. A short window is responsive: it follows changes quickly. A long window is stable: one odd month moves it less, but it lags behind real changes (slide 24).

Predict. Go to January, Year 3 and press Play. When are the 3- and 6-month averages below the actual orders, and when are they above?

Show the answer

Below in spring, while orders climb, and above in fall, while orders fall. An average only looks back, so it trails every turn. The 6-month average trails the most: its Year 3 errors run from +63.83 to +234.00 cases in March to June and from −68.17 to −146.83 cases in October to December.

Then try. Set the weights to 0.34, 0.33, 0.33, then to 0.7, 0.2, 0.1. What happens to the weighted moving average?

Show the answer

With (almost) equal weights it is the 3-month simple moving average again. Putting 0.7 on last month makes it follow the newest data more closely: more responsive. Its Year 3 MAD drops from 74.24 (weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2) to 62.80 cases. The weights must add up to 1 (slide 25); the flexibility to choose them is what makes the WMA better than the SMA (slide 30).

On the slides: exponential smoothing (slides 31–38)

Correct last month's error

Exponential smoothing needs only last month's forecast and last month's actual orders (slide 31). Next month's forecast = last forecast + α × (last month's error), where error = actual − forecast (slide 32). The chart marks last month's error; α decides how much of it the forecast corrects. Forecasting started with a given forecast of 600 cases for January, Year 1, as the slides start with a given January forecast (slide 34).

The answers below are for the starting settings: α = 0.20, our orders. brings them back.

Predict, then step. Go to June, Year 3. Last month (May) the forecast was 796.14 and 1,000 cases came in. What is June's forecast with α = 0.20?

Show the answer

May's error = 1,000 − 796.14 = 203.86. June's forecast = 796.14 + 0.20 × 203.86 = 836.91: the forecast moves only 20% of the way toward May's 1,000 cases. June then brings 1,009 cases, so June's error is +172.09. The calculation box under the chart shows the same steps for any month.

Predict. Our orders change a lot within the year. Should α be small or large? Try 0.2 and 0.8 and compare the Year 3 MAD in the table.

Show the answer

Large. With α = 0.20 the forecast trails the seasonal swings: Year 3 MAD = 100.11 cases. With α = 0.80 it follows them: MAD = 57.94. Changing demand calls for a large α (slide 37).

Then try. Switch to Steady orders (no trend, no season, only random changes). Which α is better now?

Show the answer

A small one. With nothing real to follow, a large α chases the random ups and downs: MAD = 32.25 cases at α = 0.80, against 28.04 at α = 0.20 and 27.79 at α = 0.10. Stable demand calls for a small α (slide 37).

Predict. Back to our orders with α = 0.20. Look at the arrows on the MFE and MAD tab (choose Exponential smoothing there). Is the forecast too low or too high more often?

Show the answer

Too low: 8 of the 12 months of Year 3 come in above the forecast, and the mean error (MFE) is +53.49 cases. Exponential smoothing cannot pick up a trend (slide 38): each month it corrects only part of the gap while orders keep growing. A larger α shrinks the lag but does not remove it.

On the slides: measuring accuracy, MFE and MAD (slides 47–50)

How far off were the forecasts?

To judge a forecasting method, compare its forecasts with the orders that actually came in. Each month's error = actual − forecast (slide 48). The chart shows the last 12 months (Year 3): each arrow runs from the forecast to the actual orders, red and up when the forecast was too low, blue and down when it was too high. The buttons above the chart switch to Show MFE (every error measured from zero; the dashed line is their average) and Show MAD (every error drawn above zero as its size; the bold dashed line is their average length).

  • MFE, mean forecast error = average of the errors. It shows the bias: positive means the forecasts were too low on average, negative means too high; the farther from 0, the more biased (slide 48). Up and down errors cancel.
  • MAD, mean absolute deviation = average of the |errors|, the arrow lengths. It shows how far off the forecasts are, in either direction. Lower is better (slide 49, where MAD is labeled “Randomness”).

The forecasts use the window, weights and α set on the other tabs. The answers below are for the starting settings: a 3-month simple moving average, weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, and α = 0.20. brings them back.

Predict. With the simple moving average, count the red arrows and the blue arrows. Will the MFE be positive or negative?

Show the answer

Positive. 8 arrows are red (forecast too low) and 4 are blue (too high). The red errors add up to 654.33 cases and the blue ones to 387.32, so MFE = (654.33 − 387.32) / 12 = +22.25 cases: on average the forecasts were too low: they are biased low. Orders keep growing, and an average of past months lags behind growth. Click Show MFE: the arrows now start at zero, and the dashed MFE line sits at +22.25, above zero.

Then compare. Switch between the three methods. Which has the lowest MAD, and why?

Show the answer

The weighted moving average: MAD = 74.24 cases. Then the 3-month simple moving average (86.80) and exponential smoothing with α = 0.20 (100.11). The WMA puts half its weight on last month, so it follows the seasonal climb and drop most closely; exponential smoothing with α = 0.20 corrects only 20% of each error, so it trails the most. Lower MAD means the forecasts were closer, on average, to what came in.

Then think. For the simple moving average, the MFE is much smaller than the MAD. How can that be? Click Show MFE, then Show MAD.

Show the answer

In the MFE, up and down errors cancel: May's +174.00 (orders climbing in spring) and November's −146.33 (orders dropping in fall) largely offset each other. The MAD takes absolute values, so nothing cancels: it is the average arrow length. On Show MAD, the four blue errors flip above zero as bars, and a bold dashed line appears at the MAD (86.80), well above the faint MFE line (+22.25). A small MFE does not mean accurate forecasts. MFE shows the bias, MAD shows the size of the errors (slide 47: bias errors vs. random errors). Of the three methods, exponential smoothing is the most biased: MFE = +53.49.

Challenges

  • Turn Trend and Season off (steady orders). For exponential smoothing, is a small or a large α better now?
    Show the answer

    Small. With the starting random orders, α = 0.80 gives MAD = 32.25 cases, α = 0.20 gives 28.04, and α = 0.10 gives 27.79. With nothing real to follow, reacting to a random change is a mistake (slide 37: stable demand, small α). The moving averages differ by only a case or two here, which over 12 months is luck.

  • Turn Season off but keep the trend. Can any SMA, WMA, or exponential smoothing setting get the MFE close to 0?
    Show the answer

    Not really. All of them only look back, so with steady growth they stay too low; responsive settings (short window, large α) shrink the bias but do not remove it. The starred forecast including trend* is built for exactly this case: turn it on and its MFE drops close to 0.

  • Turn Random off. Do the forecasts become perfect?
    Show the answer

    No. Without random changes the orders are perfectly predictable, but averages and smoothing still trail the trend and the season. A method that fits the pattern would be needed; these methods only react to past numbers.

* Forecast including trend: starred slides 39–45, not tested.

How this page works
  • Orders for month t (Year 1 January = month 0) = level 600 + trend × t + season + random, rounded to whole cases. Season = amplitude × cos(2π(month − 5.5)/12): highest in June and July, lowest in December and January. Random = a normal random number with mean 0 and the standard deviation you choose. Season and random are rounded to two decimals. The same random numbers are used until you click New random orders.
  • Forecasts use the formulas on the slides: simple moving average (slide 16), weighted moving average (slide 25; weights listed from the most recent month back), exponential smoothing (slide 32) starting from a given forecast of 600 for January, Year 1. A forecast is drawn only when the method has enough history: an n-month average gives its first forecast for the (n + 1)th month (a 3-month average: April, Year 1).
  • Error = actual − forecast. MFE = average error; MAD = average absolute error (slides 48–49), over the last 12 months (Year 3). Every forecast is rounded to two decimals, at every step of exponential smoothing too, so the numbers on the page can be checked by hand.
  • * Forecast including trend (starred slides 39–45, not tested): F = previous FIT + α(previous A − previous FIT); T = previous T + δ(F − previous FIT); FIT = F + T, starting from F = 600 and T = 10 for January, Year 1.
  • Practice: each new problem draws four months of orders for one store and random weights, α, and given forecasts.
