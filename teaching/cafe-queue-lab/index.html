On the slide: service rate μ > arrival rate λ

Why the barista must be faster than demand

The barista can serve μ = 20 customers per hour, and λ = 20 customers arrive per hour. Capacity matches demand exactly, like the balanced process on the "Life Without Variability" slide.

In line now–

Predict, then run. Right now there is no variability: a customer walks in every 3 minutes and every order is 3 cups, which takes 3 minutes. Switch to Real life. The averages stay the same. What happens to the line? Watch for a while, then click Long run under the café.

Show what happens

The line never settles down. In the long run the chart of people in line keeps climbing and the average time in line keeps rising; after 10,000 hours more than a hundred people are in line. Each minute the barista stands idle because nobody is there is capacity lost for good; it cannot be saved for the next rush. So capacity equal to demand on average is not enough. This is the flaw of averages, and it is why μ has to be larger than λ.

Then try. Keep Real life. Set λ to 22, just above μ, and click Long run. Then try λ = 18.

Show what happens

At λ = 22 the line explodes: it grows by about 2 people every hour (22 arrive, at most 20 are served), so after 10,000 hours about 20,000 people are waiting. At λ = 18 the barista has spare capacity to catch up after each rush, and the line goes up and down instead of growing. It is still long, about 17 minutes in line on average.

On the slide: server is busy for λ/μ of the time (utilization)

Utilization: how busy is the barista?

About λ = 10 customers arrive per hour, and the barista can serve μ = 20 per hour. So the barista should be busy λ/μ = 10/20 = 50% of the time.

Predict, then run. Raise λ to 15 per hour. What share of the time will the barista be busy? Will the time in line go up by the same 50%?

Show what happens

The barista is busy 15/20 = 75% of the time: utilization is simply λ/μ. Time in line goes up far more than 50%. In the long run it triples, from about 1.8 to about 5.5 minutes. Click Long run before and after the change to see it.

Then try. Make the barista faster: 45 seconds per cup. What is μ now?

Show what happens

An average order is 3 cups × 45 seconds = 2.25 minutes, so μ = 60 ÷ 2.25 ≈ 26.7 customers per hour. With λ = 15 the barista is busy 15/26.7 ≈ 56% of the time.

On the slide: waiting time in system = time-in-line + time-in-service

Where does a customer's time go?

Each customer's time in the café has two parts: time in line, waiting for the barista, and time at the counter, while the cups are made. Below are the last 8 customers as the day runs.

Predict, then run. With λ = 10 and μ = 20 the barista is idle half the time. Do customers still wait in line?

Show what happens

Yes. About half of the customers find the barista busy and wait, about 1.8 minutes on average in the long run. Customers arrive in bunches and some orders are big, so the idle minutes and the busy minutes come at different times.

Then try. Lower λ to 5 per hour. Which part shrinks: time in line or time at the counter?

Show what happens

Only time in line, to about 0.6 minutes in the long run. Time at the counter stays at about 3 minutes, because it depends on the order and the barista, not on how many other customers there are.

Four ways to cut the wait

Starting point: λ = 16 and μ = 20 per hour (the barista is 80% busy), random arrivals, 1 to 5 cups, no limit on the line.

  1. Click Long run to record the starting point.
  2. Change one lever below, then click Long run again.
  3. Click Reset (under the café) to go back to the starting point before you try the next lever.

Increase μ faster barista

Predict. Cut the time per cup from 60 to 45 seconds. How much does time in line drop?

Show what happens

From about 7.3 to about 2.1 minutes in the long run. Utilization falls from 80% to 60%. Near full utilization, a little extra capacity cuts waiting a lot.

Reduce λ fewer customers per hour

Predict. Lower λ from 16 to 12 per hour. What happens to time in line?

Show what happens

It falls from about 7.3 to about 2.8 minutes. The barista is 60% busy, the same as with the faster barista, but each order still takes 3 minutes at the counter.

Reduce variability same averages, less uncertainty

Predict. Keep λ = 16 and μ = 20. Make the orders less variable: 2 to 4 cups, then every customer 3 cups. Then put the orders back to 1 to 5 cups and make the arrivals Steady. Which change helps most?

Show what happens

Less variable orders help a little: time in line goes from about 7.3 minutes (1 to 5 cups) to about 6.5 (2 to 4 cups) and 6.0 (every customer 3 cups). Steady arrivals help far more, down to about 0.7 minutes, because in this café most of the waiting comes from customers arriving in bunches. More variable orders do the opposite: with exponential orders, the M/M/1 case, time in line rises to about 12 minutes.

Limit waiting space at the cost of throughput

Predict. Allow at most 5 people in line. What happens to time in line, and to the number of customers served per hour?

Show what happens

Time in line falls from about 7.3 to about 4.6 minutes, but about 3% of customers find the line full and walk away. The café serves about 15.5 customers per hour instead of 16, and the barista is busy less often. Shorter waits come at the cost of lower throughput.

Challenges

  • With λ = 16 and 1 to 5 cups, what is the slowest barista (most seconds per cup) that keeps the long-run time in line under 3 minutes?
    Show the answer

    Somewhere between 45 and 50 seconds per cup. At 45 seconds the long-run time in line is about 2.1 minutes; at 50 seconds it is just over 3.

  • Set λ to 18, then 19. The barista is only a little busier each time. What happens to the long-run time in line?
    Show the answer

    It roughly doubles each time: about 7 minutes at λ = 16, 17 at λ = 18, and 35 at λ = 19. Waiting explodes as utilization approaches 100%.

  • Set the arrivals to Random and the orders to Exponential. This is exactly the M/M/1 queue from class. Click Long run and compare the time in line with the formula Wq = λ / (μ(μ − λ)).
    Show the answer

    At λ = 16 and μ = 20 the formula gives 16 ÷ (20 × 4) = 0.2 hours = 12 minutes, and the long run gives about 11.9. The "Average time in line" chart shows the formula as a dashed line. Try other values of λ: they match too.

  • At λ = 16 with random arrivals, try all four order options and click Long run after each. How does time in line change as the orders get more variable?
    Show the answer

    About 6.0 minutes (every customer 3 cups), 6.5 (2 to 4 cups), 7.3 (1 to 5 cups), and 11.9 (exponential). The average order (3 cups) and μ never change; only the variability does.

  • Can a café keep its barista busy 100% of the time and still keep the line under control?
    Show the answer

    No. That would need zero variability: every customer arriving exactly on schedule and every order exactly the same. Real service always has variability, and with λ = μ the line then grows without limit (try λ = 20 with Real life on the first tab and click Long run). That is why service capacity must exceed demand, and why a well-run service has some idle time.

How this simulation works
  • One barista and one line. Customers are served first come, first served.
  • Every order option averages 3 cups, and each cup takes the barista's time per cup (60 seconds unless you change it). So an average order takes 3 minutes and μ = 20 customers per hour, whatever the order option.
  • Orders "every customer 3 cups", "2 to 4 cups" and "1 to 5 cups": each cup takes exactly the barista's time per cup, so a customer's service time is the number of cups times that time.
  • Orders "Exponential": the number of cups is 1, 2, 3, … with each extra cup less likely (1 cup 33%, 2 cups 22%, 3 cups 15%, and so on), and each cup takes a random time that averages the barista's time per cup. Together this makes the service time exponential with a mean of 3 minutes, the assumption behind the M/M/1 formulas.
  • Random arrivals: the time between customers is exponential with mean 60/λ minutes (a Poisson process, as in the course slides). Steady arrivals: exactly one customer every 60/λ minutes.
  • Line space counts the people waiting, not the customer at the counter. When the line is full, a new customer walks away.
  • The two buttons under the picture add customers on top of the regular arrivals: 5 at once, or one customer with a 10-cup order.
  • "Replay this day" restarts from time zero with the same settings and the same customers. "New random day" restarts with the same settings and new random customers. "Reset" goes back to the current tab's starting settings and restarts.
  • Long run simulates 10,000 hours of business with the current settings and the same random numbers every time. When λ is not below μ, the line grows without limit; the long run still runs, so you can watch it explode.
